Healthcare.

Better Care. Better Access. Better Lives.

We have the knowledge, talent and resources to make healthcare more dependable. The challenge is designing systems that put those capabilities within reach, while navigating the cost and complexity of transforming an enormous industry.

Page 01 / The Possibility

Healthcare Worth Building.

Imagine being able to find timely care, understand the cost, trust the guidance, and stay connected to a team that knows you. This is the standard we can work toward, whether people live in a downtown neighborhood or a small rural town.

The story so far

Powerful medicine. Uneven access.

American medicine has produced extraordinary advances in emergency treatment, surgery, medications, diagnostics and research. Yet people can struggle to afford routine care, navigate insurance or find a clinician who can stay with them over time.

In 2024, about 91.8% of people had insurance, according to CMS. Coverage matters, but insurance alone cannot guarantee affordability, continuity, coordination or good outcomes.

What better looks like

Care that stays connected.

Accessible primary care. Financial protection. Strong specialty and emergency services. Prevention and rehabilitation. Reliable follow-up. A voice for patients in the choices that shape their lives.

Reach

Care nearby

Bring routine, preventive and ongoing care within practical reach of more people.

Trust

Someone knows you

Make continuity and clear communication fundamental parts of care.

Outcome

Better lives

Measure whether care improves health, function, well-being and the ability to live meaningfully.

The destination is clear: dependable care that helps people live better lives. The path requires evidence, investment and deliberate choices.

Page 02 / The Obstacles

Five Trillion Dollars of Momentum.

Changing the incentives and operations of American healthcare means working across insurers, health systems, employers, governments, clinicians, patients, research, technology and labor markets. Millions depend on the current arrangements, even when those arrangements are frustrating.

Obstacle 01

Incentives follow payment.

Revenue depends on contracts, prices, utilization and billing rules. Changes in one part of the system affect staffing, facilities, insurance premiums, reimbursement and access elsewhere. Institutions need workable transitions, not merely ambitious targets.

Obstacle 02

Fragmentation consumes effort.

Patients and clinicians navigate separate networks, records, authorizations and payment systems. Improving coordination requires technology, trust, governance and the capacity to sustain a relationship beyond an isolated visit.

A closer look / the 90% problem

What does the statistic actually measure?

The CDC says that 90% of annual spending is for people with chronic and mental health conditions. That wording describes who incurred expenses. It does not mean that 90% of spending is specifically to treat those conditions.

90%

Spending attributed to people classified as having chronic conditions in the underlying RAND analysis of 2014 survey data.

≠ 90%

Share of national expenditures proven to be direct chronic-disease treatment, or spending shown to be avoidable.

Example: Someone with controlled high cholesterol breaks an ankle. Their fracture treatment counts as spending on a person with a chronic condition. It does not become cholesterol treatment.

A second measurement issue: the original RAND study and the much broader CMS national spending accounts use different spending populations and accounting scopes. Applying the old percentage directly to today's total does not produce a new measurement of chronic-disease treatment.

Chronic conditions still matter enormously. Accurate attribution helps us target interventions, assess outcomes and avoid exaggerated savings assumptions.

Obstacle 03

Metrics can misdirect.

A dataset can be accurate for its intended purpose and misleading when used to answer a different question. Effective reform distinguishes spending on people, spending on specific conditions, avoidable complications, administrative costs, and improvements in quality of life.

Other constraints include rural workforce shortages, geographic inequality, investment timelines, disagreement over financing and the difficulty of demonstrating long-term returns.

Changing the Ledger.

A national healthcare reform cannot be evaluated solely by its sticker price. We have to look at who pays now, who would pay after a change, the transition costs, what people gain, and which savings are realistic.

Potential gains

What investment may buy

Earlier care, fewer avoidable complications, reduced financial exposure, better function and health, improved workforce stability, and more predictable care relationships. Each claim requires a defined population, timeframe and measurement plan.

Transition costs

What change requires

Clinical staffing, training, financing, upgraded information systems, additional primary care capacity, redesigned payment contracts and protection of essential services during transition.

Four questions for any proposal

Who pays?

Households, employers, insurers, state and federal governments.

Who gains?

Patients, workers, clinicians, communities, businesses.

When?

Upfront investments may precede measurable savings by years.

What changes?

Total spending, its distribution, outcomes, administrative burden or all four?

A proposed program is financially incomplete without a baseline, implementation budget, funding source, transition plan and explicit uncertainty range.

Page 04 / What Works

Models Worth Learning From.

Working examples make the future tangible. None solves every problem. The useful question is which mechanisms succeed, for whom, under what conditions, and at what cost.

Coverage and financing

Different routes to wider access

Other high-income nations achieve broad coverage using different mixtures of public and private insurance and varying methods of paying clinicians. Their experiences offer evidence about access, financial protection and administrative design.

Compare actual benefits, capacity, taxes, premiums, waiting times and outcomes.

Primary care and continuity

Care before crisis

Team-based primary care, coordinated follow-up, medication management and behavioral health integration are promising approaches to reducing fragmentation and supporting complex patients. Their effectiveness depends on how programs are implemented and evaluated.

Whole-person care

What matters to the patient

Some programs broaden measurement beyond diagnosis or service volume to include function, goals, well-being and patient experience. Compare quality-of-life outcomes alongside safety, utilization and cost.

Local ownership and experimentation

Community-governed models

Cooperatives and other locally governed care arrangements can test relationships among access, payment, accountability and patient voice. They need transparent reporting, adequate funding and independent evaluation.

PrimaryCare.Solutions ↗ is one proposed community-owned primary-care framework, presented as a developing model rather than independently validated national proof.

Real solutions are specific enough to be tested and humble enough to be improved.

Page 05 / Make It Happen

Build What Comes Next.

People can influence the future of healthcare through local institutions, public deliberation, professional work, research, community organizing and the questions they bring to decision-makers.

Start with the experience.

Ask people what reliably works in their care and where they lose time, money, agency or continuity. Protect privacy and seek experiences from people who are often underrepresented.

Learn how care is financed locally.

Review employer benefits, hospital community-benefit reports, state health budgets and public insurance policy. Ask how an investment changes costs and outcomes over time.

Find work already underway.

Explore health centers, local coalitions, rural programs, professional associations, cooperatives, and community research projects. Ask what evidence they collect and where they need help.

Bring five questions to every proposal.

What problem does it solve? Who benefits? Who pays? What evidence supports it? How will we know if lives improve?

Measure, share and improve.

Define a small attainable goal. Track meaningful outcomes, disclose limitations, publish results and adjust the model as evidence develops.
