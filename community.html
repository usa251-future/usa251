A Place to Call Home.

Home is a place to live, people we can count on, and the opportunity to help shape the community we share.

01 / THE POSSIBILITY

Where Life Takes Place

We experience America in homes, on blocks, in libraries, parks, businesses, schools, places of worship, and gathering places. The quality of these everyday settings shapes opportunity and belonging.

Home

Stable, safe and attainable housing throughout different stages of life.

Place

Neighborhoods, civic spaces, local services and environments that support daily life.

Connection

Relationships, cultural exchange, participation, and trust that make people feel at home.

Agency

A meaningful voice in the decisions that shape where we live.

The outcome: A place where people can establish a life, contribute, and create opportunities for those who follow.
02 / THE OBSTACLES

What Makes Belonging Harder

Community life is shaped by housing costs, planning rules, access to services, infrastructure, local budgets, and whether people have a practical way to participate in decisions.

Housing supply and fit

Land-use rules, construction costs, available land, infrastructure and financing affect what homes can be built and who can afford them.

Disconnection

Distance, transportation barriers, inaccessible buildings and fragmented services can make ordinary activities difficult.

Uneven capacity

Different communities have vastly different tax bases, staffing, local institutions and available investment.

Public trust

Residents need clear information about choices, costs and decision authority, with follow-through they can observe.

03 / THE INVESTMENT

What It Takes to Build Home

Housing, infrastructure, public space and community services require capital. Maintaining them requires durable financing, capable local institutions and decisions about what gets prioritized.

What we pay for

Land, construction, utilities, accessibility, maintenance, public facilities, staffing and financing.

Where money comes from

Households, businesses, local taxes, state and federal programs, philanthropy and community investment.

Tradeoffs

Density versus space, near-term affordability versus long-run maintenance, new construction versus rehabilitation, and changing land use versus preserving valued places.

What to measure

Housing stability, cost burden, access to essential places, quality of public spaces and participation over time.

For each proposed project, use the actual local capital budget and operating forecast. National cost averages alone cannot establish local feasibility.
04 / WHAT WORKS

Places Worth Learning From

Some of the most practical community-building tools already exist. The challenge is selecting approaches suited to local needs and evaluating how they perform.

Local comprehensive plans

City and county plans connect land use, housing, public facilities, transportation and community priorities.

Community land trusts

Locally governed land stewardship can support long-term affordability under appropriate legal and financing conditions.

Libraries and shared spaces

Public libraries, parks, civic centers and markets give people places to learn, gather and organize.

Community-led projects

Neighborhood partnerships, cooperatives and improvement initiatives can turn existing relationships into sustained work.

These are starting categories for future sourced case studies, not claims that every implementation succeeds. We will add documented examples and results as this exhibit develops.

05 / MAKE IT HAPPEN

Make Your Place Better

Start where decisions are already being made. The plans, budgets and public meetings of a city, county, school district or housing authority often show where a specific proposal can move forward.

  1. Find the plan. Locate your city or county comprehensive plan, housing plan, capital improvement program and current budget.
  2. Identify the decision. Is the issue zoning, a public building, a housing project, a park, a transportation connection or a service? Find the body with authority.
  3. Bring a specific proposal. Name the location, problem, supporting information, expected benefit, estimated cost and funding question.
  4. Join the process. Contact the relevant planning commission, council, local organization or project team before a decision is final.
  5. Follow the outcome. Request timelines, milestones and public reporting. Help celebrate progress and learn from setbacks.
A useful question to bring to a representative: “Which existing plan and budget line addresses this need, what has delayed progress, and what decision can move it forward this year?”
